## Five things that matter more than being polished

You are new at this. These five decide whether the day works.

**1. Do not slow down the handover.** Every instinct will say speak slowly and clearly. Fight it. If you slow down, everyone succeeds and nobody learns anything.

**2. The ten seconds of silence is real.** After the handover you say nothing for ten seconds. It feels unbearable to you and normal to them, because they are writing.

**3. You are allowed to be imperfect.** Stumble over a ticket number and keep going. Real handovers have stumbles in them.

**4. Say people's names when they get it right.** *"Ana just repeated the hostname back before agreeing. That is exactly the skill."* Trainees copy whatever gets named out loud.

**5. If you get lost, say so.** *"Give me a second, I have lost my place."* Nobody thinks less of you. Pretending is worse.

## SLIDE 8 — THE ALERT PIPELINE · 9:45–10:00

Before I show you the technical version of this, let me describe a shopping mall.

A mall has cameras. Dozens of them. Each camera sees one small area — one entrance, one corridor, one section of the car park. No single camera sees the whole mall.

All of those cameras feed into one room, where there is a wall of monitors. That room collects everything and puts it in one place.

And in that room sits a guard. The guard watches. Most of what appears on those screens is nothing — shoppers walking, a child running, a delivery arriving. But occasionally the guard sees something that is not right, and has to make a decision: is this real, or is it nothing?

If it is real, the guard does something. Radios a supervisor. Calls the police. Writes it in the logbook.

That is the job. That is the entire job. Now let me give you the technical words for the same thing.

Sensors are the cameras. Antivirus on a laptop. The firewall at the edge of the network. Software watching what programs are doing on a computer. Each one sees a slice, and not one of them sees everything.

The SIEM is the monitor room. It is a long name — Security Information and Event Management — and nobody ever says the long version out loud. It collects what all the sensors report, puts it in one place, and ranks it so the important things are near the top. Without a SIEM you have ten tools each shouting in a different room. With a SIEM, you have one room.

The analyst is the guard. That is you. The system says "this looks interesting." You decide whether it is real.

And I want to stop on that one, because it is the whole reason this job exists. A tool cannot do this part. A tool can tell you that something is unusual. It cannot tell you whether it matters. Unusual and important are two different things, and telling them apart is what you are being paid for.

Action is the radio call. A ticket. An escalation. A phone call to a client at two in the morning.

And then record. Write down what you did and why. Six months from now, when somebody asks "why did you do that?", your note is the only answer that exists. If you did not write it down, it did not happen. I will say that sentence a lot over fifteen days.

## SLIDE 9 — THE TIERS · 10:00–10:15

A security team is organised in tiers, and the closest thing to it is a hospital emergency room.

When you walk into an emergency room, the first person you see is the triage nurse. That nurse does not perform your surgery. Their job is to look at you quickly, work out how serious it is, and send you to the right place. They see everybody, and they see them first.

Then there is the doctor who actually treats you. Then a specialist, for the complicated cases. And somewhere upstairs there is a hospital director who decides things like whether to close a ward.

A security team works exactly the same way.

Level One is the triage nurse. That is you, after this course. You watch the queue, you look at each alert, you work out how serious it is, you write it up, and you pass it on.

Level Two takes what you escalated and investigates it properly. "It has spread to four machines. We are containing it."

Level Three hunts for the things nobody alerted on at all. Small teams. Years of experience. You will meet them eventually.

The SOC Manager runs the desk. Staffing, client relationships, and the big decisions.

Now — the most important sentence on this slide, and I would like you to write it down.

Notice what Level One does not do. You do not decide to disconnect a company from the internet. You do not rebuild a server. You do not talk to the press. Knowing the edge of your authority is a competency, and an assessor will test you on it.

Let me tell you why this matters, because it is not about following rules for their own sake.

Imagine you are three weeks into your first job. It is one in the morning. A client calls you directly, and they are panicking, and they say: "Please, just disconnect that laptop from the network. Right now."

Every instinct you have says help this person. They are upset. It sounds urgent. You could do it in thirty seconds.

Do not do it.

Not because the client is wrong — they might be completely right. But you do not know what else is running on that machine. You do not know whether disconnecting it destroys the evidence your Level Two colleague needs an hour from now. You do not know whether it is a production server that takes the payroll system down with it.

What you say instead is: "I do not have the authority to do that. I am escalating this to my manager right now, and they will call you back." And then you escalate immediately — you do not just say it to get off the phone.

That is not being unhelpful. That is being professional. And the person who does that in their first month is the person who gets promoted in their first year.

## SLIDE 10 — THE SHIFT HANDOVER · 10:25–10:35

Welcome back. This next hour is the part of the morning that matters most.

If you have ever had a relative in hospital, you may have noticed something at shift change. The nurse going off duty stands with the nurse coming on, and they go through every patient. What medication. What time. What to watch for. Who is worried about what.

They call it the endorsement. And hospitals take it extremely seriously, because it is the single most dangerous moment in a hospital day. Not the surgery. The handover. That is where information gets dropped.

A security operations centre has exactly the same moment, for exactly the same reason. Every shift begins and ends with a handover, and it has a standard shape. Five sections. Always the same five, always in the same order.

Open items. Tickets still in progress, with the ticket number and where it currently stands.

Escalated. What went up to Level Two or Three, and who owns it now. This one matters because if it has been escalated, you must not touch it. Somebody else is working on it and two people working the same incident is worse than one.

Watch items. Things that are not incidents yet, but need eyes on them. This is the section people forget most often, and it is frequently the one that turns into your night.

System status. Anything down, broken, or under maintenance.

Client notes. Anything a client asked for or complained about.

Same five sections, every shift, everywhere in the world. Learn them today and you will use them for your entire career.

## SLIDE 11 — THE READ-BACK RULE · 10:50–11:00

You already know this rule. You have seen it done a thousand times and never once thought about it.

When you order at a fast food counter, what does the crew member do before they charge you? They repeat your order back. "One Chickenjoy, one rice, large drink — correct?"

Why do they do that? It costs four seconds. Multiply four seconds by every customer, every day, across every branch in the country — that is an enormous amount of time to spend.

They do it because getting the order wrong costs far more than four seconds. Wrong food, remake it, angry customer, wasted product, queue backing up.

That is the read-back rule. That is all it is. And in this job, the wrong order is not a burger. It is the wrong computer.

Here is what it sounds like at work.

"So you want me to isolate WKS-042 — not WKS-024 — and notify the IT manager. Confirming."

Three things in every good read-back.

One: the identifier, said back exactly. The machine name, the address, the ticket number. This is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters most. WKS-042 and WKS-024 are two different computers, in two different offices, belonging to two different people. One of them is having a bad night. The other one is about to.

Two: the action. What you are actually being asked to do.

Three: the notification. Who you are going to tell. This is the part that gets dropped most often, and then nobody informs the client, and the client finds out from somebody else. That is a bad phone call.

Four seconds. Pilots do it. Nurses do it. Air traffic controllers do it. They all do it for the same reason — the cost of being wrong is very high, and human hearing is simply not reliable.

One more thing, and this is the honest part. The mistake is not saying "okay." Saying "okay" feels polite and efficient and cooperative. And it confirms absolutely nothing. The person who gave you the instruction learns nothing at all about what you actually heard.

"Okay" is the most dangerous word in this job.

Let me show you what that looks like, and I need a volunteer.

## SLIDE 12 — WHEN TO STOP AND ASK · 11:12–11:25

Three situations mean stop and ask. Only three, so they are easy to remember.

One: you do not understand the instruction.
Two: the instruction contradicts the written procedure.
Three: doing it would go beyond your authority.

In all three, asking is correct. Not asking is the mistake.

Now let me say the thing that is actually going through your head.

You are new. Your supervisor sounds busy, or annoyed, or both. You did not quite catch the machine name. And there is a voice saying: if I ask him to repeat that, he will think I was not listening. He will think I am slow. Better to just guess — it is probably the one from earlier.

I understand that completely. Every single person in this industry has had that thought. Let me tell you how it ends.

You guess. You act on the wrong machine. Two hours later somebody works out what happened.

And now the conversation is not "sorry, could you repeat that" — which is a four-second conversation that nobody remembers by lunchtime. It is "why did you take the finance director's laptop off the network at two in the morning." That is a conversation that goes to a manager, and it goes in writing.

Asking makes you look careful. Guessing makes you look careless. Every experienced person in this industry knows that. The only people who think asking looks weak are people who have not done the job.

So here are four sentences. Steal them. Genuinely — write them on a sticky note and put it on the edge of your monitor.

"Can you repeat the hostname, the IP, or the ticket number?"

"Do you want that now, or after I finish the current ticket?"

"That is above my authority — should I escalate it, or are you authorising it?"

"The procedure says X and you are asking for Y. Which do you want me to follow?"

That last one is the most powerful sentence on this slide. It is polite. It is professional. And it protects you completely, because you are not refusing anything — you are asking which instruction to follow. Whatever they answer, you write it down, along with who said it and when.

Asking is not weakness. Not asking is the error.

## SLIDE 13 — THE SHIFT HUDDLE · 11:25–11:30

A huddle is a short team meeting at the start of a shift. Four minutes. Not a meeting where somebody presents slides — a meeting where everyone speaks once and then you go to work.

If you have worked in a call centre, or a restaurant, or retail, you have been in one. The supervisor gathers everybody before the shift, says what matters tonight, everyone says where they are, and it is finished before anyone gets comfortable.

Four steps.

The Shift Lead opens and says what the priorities are.

Each member reports their open items. One sentence each. Not a story. One sentence.

The team agrees who takes the watch item, by name. Not "someone should look at that." A name.

And the Shift Lead closes with the single thing that cannot slip tonight.

In an office, everybody stands up for a huddle, and standing is what keeps it short — nobody wants to stand for twenty minutes. On a call we cannot do that, so the timer does that job instead.

One rule, and it is the graded one. Everyone speaks. If a member of your team says nothing, the huddle has failed, no matter how good it sounded. This unit is about contributing to a group. Silence is not contribution, and I cannot write down that I observed you contributing if you did not say anything.

## SLIDE 17 — READING AN SOP · 12:00–12:04

This slide is a tool for this afternoon. Take a screenshot of it.

A procedure document is written very carefully, and the important words are the small ones. Five words tell you what you are actually obliged to do.

Shall, and must, mean mandatory. No discretion. You do it.

Should means expected. You can deviate, but you had better be able to explain why afterwards.

May means it is your judgement — and there is almost always a condition attached, usually "provided you document it."

Shall not means you have no authority at all. That is the edge of your role.

And "within X minutes" means a clock is running. Your job is to know exactly when that clock starts.

That last one is the whole of question one. That is your hint, and it is a generous one.

Let me show you why the difference matters, with one line.

"The analyst may close a low-severity alert provided the assessment is documented."

Read that quickly and you hear: I am allowed to close low alerts. Fine. Moving on.

Read it properly and you see that the permission has a price attached. You may close it — if you write down why. Close it without writing anything, and you did not use your discretion. You breached the procedure.

That is the difference between reading a document and reading it properly. This afternoon, read properly.

## SLIDE 18 — ADVISORY VERSUS CHANGE NOTICE · 12:04–12:07

Two kinds of document land on your desk, and they do different jobs. Think of two memos on an office noticeboard.

The first one says: there is a scam going around, people are getting text messages pretending to be from the bank. That is an advisory. It tells you a threat exists. Usually it is information, occasionally it needs action.

The second one says: the lifts will be switched off this Saturday from eight in the morning until noon, for maintenance. That is a change notice. It tells you a system will be modified at a specific time.

Now — which of those two saves you the most work on a night shift?

The second one. And it is not close.

Scheduled change is the number one cause of false alerts.

If you do not read the change notices, you will spend an entire night investigating an attack that is actually the IT team patching servers exactly as they said they would. This happens to every new analyst precisely once, and it is a long and embarrassing night, and you never forget to check again.

One more thing, and this is really the whole point of your Task Six. A notice tells you what it does not cover, just as clearly as what it does. If the notice lists four servers in Manila, and a server in Cebu goes down — that is not covered. That is not scheduled maintenance. That is an incident. Watch for that this afternoon.

## DEMO 1 — THE SPOKEN HANDOVER
#### 10:35–10:50 · 15 minutes

### What this is

You read out a shift handover, once, at normal speed. They write down what they can. Then you show them what was actually said and they see the gaps.

### Why it matters more than anything else today

This cannot be done any other way. It cannot be a worksheet, it cannot be homework, and it cannot be caught up later. **If this demo does not happen, that part of the unit does not happen.** If your morning is running late, take the time from somewhere else.

### Before you start

- [ ] The script is **on paper** in front of you
- [ ] You know which hostname you are mumbling
- [ ] Everyone has a pen and paper — ask them, do not assume

### STEP 1 — Stop sharing your screen

**DO:** Stop the screen share. Completely. Camera on, screen off.

> **SAY:**
> "I am taking the slide down now. You have to do this by ear, and if the slide stays up you will just copy the five headings off it and learn nothing."

**Why this matters:** if the handover-structure slide is still on screen, they copy the five section headings and fill them in. You will have tested their reading, not their listening. This is the single most common way this demo fails.

---

### STEP 2 — Set it up

> **SAY:**
> "Get a pen and paper. Not a document on your computer — paper, if you have it.
>
> Here is the situation. It is nine o'clock at night. You have just arrived for your shift. The analyst who is going home is about to hand over to you. I am going to say it once, at normal speed. I am not going to slow down, because in real life nobody slows down for you.

**DO:** Do not answer questions at this point. If someone asks "how long is it?" — say "you will find out," and start.

### STEP 3 — Read the script

**DO:** Read it at normal conversational pace. Mumble the one hostname you chose. Do not explain anything as you go.

>
> [Name] — read me back what you wrote.
>
> [Name] — what did you get?
>
> [Name] — and you?"

### STEP 6 — Show them the real answer

**DO:** Share your screen. Put this table up.

| Section | What was actually said |
|---------|----------------------|

#### Question 1 — "Who wrote down all three ticket numbers?"

> ## ANSWER: **4471, 4478, 4482**

- **4478** — Phishing in Finance

Because you deliberately reuse a hostname from the handover fifteen minutes ago — and the number is slightly different. Almost nobody notices. That moment does more teaching than any explanation.

### Before you start

- [ ] You said `WKS-042` in the handover
- [ ] You will now say `WKS-024` — **the digits are swapped**. Check you have this the right way round before you speak.

> **SAY:**
> "Before you act on an instruction that matters, you repeat it back in your own words and wait for confirmation. That is the whole rule."

Use the list below — it does not matter which order.

teach. Keep it moving — you want everybody to get a turn.

**Eight or nine trainees, about 45 seconds each, is ten minutes.**

#### YOUR INSTRUCTIONS — read one to each trainee

| # | Read this out loud | Watch for |

| 1 | "Check WKS-207 for the quarantine failure — not WKS-270 — and tell the IT manager before midnight." | Do they say **207**, not 270? |

| 3 | "Do not close ticket 3402. Level 2 is still on it." | Do they keep the word **not**? |

| 8 | "Ticket 3390 needs a client call before end of shift. It is High severity." | Do they say the **ticket number**? |
| 9 | "Two things: chase the Northwind report, and hand over to the day shift by five." | Do they get **both**? |

#### WHAT A GOOD ANSWER SOUNDS LIKE

Using instruction 1:

> *"Confirming — WKS-207, not 270, checking the quarantine failure, and I tell the IT manager
> before midnight. Is that right?"*
>
> *"Should I tell them by email or by phone?"*

#### YOUR ONE SENTENCE OF FEEDBACK

Pick whichever fits and move on:

- **They got it:** *"Good — you said the machine name back. That is the whole skill."*
- **They dropped who to tell:** *"You got the action but not who to tell. That is the half people always lose."*
- **They said 'okay':** *"That is what most people do, and it confirms nothing. Try it again — say the machine name back to me."*
- **They asked no question:** *"Read-back was good. Now ask me something — anything."*
- **They got the wrong number:** *"I said 207, you said 270. Different machine, different office. That is exactly why we do this."*

**Say people's names when they get it right.** Trainees copy whatever gets named out loud.

#### TICK THEM OFF AS YOU GO

Keep a list of names and tick each one. That tick is your evidence for this element.

**Anybody you do not get to, run at the start of Day 2.** Do not let a trainee reach Day 15
without doing this once while you watched.

### IF IT GOES WRONG

| What happens | What you do |
|---|---|
| **The first trainee reads it back correctly instead of saying "okay"** | Praise them, then ask the class: *"How many of you would have just said okay? Be honest."* Hands go up. The point still lands. |
| **A trainee freezes and says nothing** | Feed them the words. *"Try starting with: confirming, WKS-207..."* This is a demonstration, not a test. Nobody fails it. |
| **They just repeat your sentence word for word** | Accept it, then say: *"That works, but say it in your own words next time — parroting catches mishearing, not misunderstanding."* |
| **You are running out of time** | Stop at whoever you have reached. Note the remaining names and run them at the start of Day 2. |
| **Somebody cannot think of a question** | Give them one: *"Try 'by email or by phone?'"* Then move on. |

### How you know it worked

**You ticked off every name on your list.** That is it.

The read-backs being clumsy is fine — first attempt, hour and a half in. What matters is that
every trainee said an instruction back to you out loud while you listened.

### PHASE 1 · 11:30 — You show them one · 3 minutes

They have never seen a huddle. If you send them straight in with no model, you get four rambling meetings and nobody learns anything.

**You have two ways to do this. Pick one now.**

#### Option A — Read it aloud yourself *(recommended for your first time)*

**WHAT YOU DO:** Open `Huddle_Drill_Pack.md`, Part 3. Read the whole dialogue out loud, playing all four people. Change your voice slightly for each one, or just say the name before each line.

No volunteers. Nothing can go wrong. Takes 90 seconds.

**WHAT YOU SAY first:**

**WHAT YOU SAY after:**

**WHAT YOU SAY:**

Only three things. Write them down as you go — you will not remember afterwards.

1. **Did every member speak?** Tick each name as they talk.
2. **Was the watch item given to a named person?**

**WHAT YOU SAY:**

#### The Alert Intake SOP

```
ALERT INTAKE SOP — SOP-SOC-001 rev 4

1.  On receipt of a detection alert, the analyst SHALL record the alert
    ID, source system, timestamp, and affected host within 5 minutes.

2.  The analyst SHALL assess the alert against the Severity Matrix
    (SOP-SOC-002) before any other action.

3.  Where the alert is assessed as Critical or High, the analyst MUST
    notify the client contact within 15 minutes of assessment.

4.  The analyst SHOULD check for a matching Change Notice before
    escalating. Where a Change Notice explains the alert, the analyst
    SHALL record the change reference and close the alert as expected
    activity.

5.  The analyst MAY close an alert assessed as Low without escalation,
    provided the assessment is documented.

6.  The analyst SHALL NOT take containment action on any host.
    Containment is authorised by the SOC Manager only.
```

**Questions to ask, step by step:**

1. **Step 1** — "What exactly is required, and what is the clock?" *(Four fields. Five minutes from receipt.)*
2. **Step 2** — "What must happen before anything else?" *(Severity assessment. Not investigation, not a phone call.)*
3. **Step 3** — "What is the deadline, and when does it start?" *(15 minutes from **assessment**, not from receipt. **This is the one everybody gets wrong.** Spend time here.)*
4. **Step 4** — "'Should' check, then 'shall' record. What is the difference?" *(You are expected to check; if you do not, justify it. If a change explains it, recording is mandatory.)*
5. **Step 5** — "What does 'may' give you?" *(Discretion — but only with documentation.)*
6. **Step 6** — "What does this remove from you?" *(Authority to contain. The boundary of the L1 role, written down.)*

### TASK 5 — THE FOUR QUESTIONS

#### Q1. An alert arrives at 22:00. You assess it as High at 22:20. What is your notification deadline, and why?

> ## ANSWER: **22:35**

The clock in §3.3 runs from **assessment**, not from receipt. Assessment was 22:20, plus
15 minutes, equals 22:35.

They were already late on §3.1 — that required recording within 5 minutes of receipt, so by
22:05. **But being late on 3.1 does not move the 3.3 deadline.** Two separate clocks.

> **This is the one most of the class gets wrong**, and the usual wrong answer is 22:15 —
> they start the clock at receipt. Spend real time here. Ask: *"Read 3.3 out loud. What word
> comes immediately before the words 'the analyst must notify'?"*
>
> It is also the single most common cause of missed SLAs in real operations, so say that.
> It makes the pedantry feel worth it.

#### Q2. You assess an alert as Low and close it without writing anything down. Which step did you breach, and what exactly was missing?

> ## ANSWER: **Step 3.5.** The missing thing is the **documented assessment**.

"May close" is conditional on "provided the assessment is documented." The permission and the
condition are in the same sentence.

**Undocumented discretion is not discretion.** If you cannot show the reasoning, you did not
exercise judgement — you just closed a ticket.

#### Q3. A client phones you directly and asks you to disconnect an infected laptop from the network. What do you do, and which step tells you that?

> ## ANSWER: **Refuse and escalate. Step 3.6.**

The words to use:

> *"I do not have the authority to do that. I am escalating this to the SOC Manager now and
> they will call you back."*

Then escalate immediately — not just say it to end the call.

> **Watch for the trainees who wanted to say yes.** That instinct is good and it needs
> redirecting, not crushing. Say so out loud: *"Wanting to help is right. Doing it yourself
> is the part that is wrong."*

#### Q4. A Change Notice explains an alert, so you close it. What must you record before you do, and which step makes it mandatory?

> ## ANSWER: **The change reference.** Step 3.4 makes it **shall**, not should.

The trap in 3.4 is that it contains both words. Checking for a notice is **should**.
Recording the reference once you have found one is **shall**.

> ## ANSWER: **Incident.**

Cebu is not in the change notice. The notice lists Manila hosts only.

> **This is the whole point of the exercise.** A notice tells you what it does **not** cover
> just as clearly as what it does. Ask the class: *"How many of you saw 'servers going
> offline is expected tonight' and applied that to all servers?"* Several hands will go up,
> and that is the lesson.

#### C. The Northwind SLA notice tells you to do something different from SOP-SOC-001 §3.3. Which do you follow?

> ## ANSWER: **Follow the Northwind notice.**

It explicitly says *"This supersedes… FOR THIS CLIENT ONLY."* **A document that has the
authority to override says so.** That sentence is the answer.

> **This is the best twenty minutes available in the whole debrief.**
>
> The correct behaviour when two documents conflict is **not** to pick the stricter one, or
> the newer one, or the one you prefer. It is to check whether the notice **has the authority
> to override** — and if you cannot tell, to ask.
>
> Anyone who answered "follow the stricter one" reasoned sensibly and still got it wrong.
> **Those are the most useful people to hear from.** Ask one of them to explain their
> thinking to the class before you give the answer. It is a far better lesson coming from a
> trainee than from you.
