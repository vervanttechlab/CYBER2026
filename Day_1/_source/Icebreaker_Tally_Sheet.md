## "The Incident You Already Survived" · 8:35–9:00

## THE THREE CODES

Every answer gets one letter. Decide it as they speak — do not overthink it.

| Code | Means | Sounds like |
|------|-------|------------|
| **S** | A **system** told them | "I got an SMS from the bank." "My antivirus popped up." "Google said someone logged in from another country." |
| **M** | They noticed it **themselves** | "My phone got really slow." "My files had weird names." "There was money missing." |
| **P** | Another **person** told them | "My friend messaged me and asked why I was sending links." "IT called me." |

Add a tick in the **Late?** column if they only found out **after** the damage was done — money already gone, account already lost, files already encrypted.

> Almost every answer is M or P. That is the whole point of the exercise, and it is what makes the closing line land.

## WHAT TO SAY WHEN YOU REVEAL IT

Point at the **S column** first.

> "Look at this column. This is 'a security system told me.' Three out of fourteen.
>
> Now look at these two." *(point at M and P)* "Eleven of you found out because something looked wrong, or because another person told you.
>
> That is exactly the job you are training for. A Level One analyst is the person who notices, and the person people come and tell. The tools help. But the noticing is the job."

Then point at the **Late? ticks**.

> "And look at these. These are the ones where nobody knew until the money was gone, or the account was already lost. Every one of those had a gap — hours, sometimes days — where it was happening and nobody was watching.
>
> Closing that gap is what a security operations centre is for. That is the whole product."

Then circle the **top two rows by total**.

> "By Day Ten you will be able to detect both of those on a real system, write them up, and escalate them to the right manager. Fifteen days."

## RUNNING IT ONLINE — FOUR PRACTICAL NOTES

**Show the call order on screen before you start.** People relax when they know they are seventh and not next.

**Do not tally on the shared screen while someone is still speaking.** Listen, then mark it after they finish. A trainer typing while a nervous person tells a story reads as not listening, and it is the first impression of the whole course.

**If somebody has genuinely never had an incident**, put a dash in the Code column and note it. Ken in the example is not a gap in the data — he is likely one of your stronger trainees, and now you know to pair him with a beginner in team formation fifteen minutes later.

**Keep the roster after today.** On Day 7 you teach phishing, and you can say "Ana, this is the one that got you — tell them what the message looked like." Trainees remember the day the course used their own story. It costs you nothing and it is the cheapest engagement you will ever buy.
